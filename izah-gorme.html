How do the eyes see?

4 min read · AXON

Light hits your eye, but you see with your brain. The journey from retina to cortex, step by step.

Into the eye

Light passes through the cornea and the lens, which focus it onto the retina at the back of the eye. The image on the retina is actually upside down — the brain turns it the right way round.

Rods and cones

The retina holds about 120 million rods, which work in dim light, and about 6 million cones, which see colour and fine detail and are packed into a small central spot called the fovea. Where the optic nerve leaves the eye there are no receptors at all — your blind spot. You do not notice it because the brain fills the gap in.

The road to the cortex

Signals leave each eye through the optic nerve. At the optic chiasm, the fibres from the nose-side half of each retina cross to the other side, so the left half of the brain sees the right half of the world. After a relay in the lateral geniculate body of the thalamus, the signal reaches the primary visual cortex (V1) at the back of the brain, in the occipital lobe.

Seeing is building

Neurons in V1 respond to simple features like edges at a particular angle — a discovery that won David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel a Nobel Prize. Later areas combine these pieces into shapes, faces, colour and motion. That is why damage to different parts of the brain can take away very specific parts of vision.
