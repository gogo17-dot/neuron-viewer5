How do MRI and CT work?

4 min read · AXON

Two machines that let doctors see inside the skull — one uses X-rays, the other a giant magnet.

CT: X-rays from every angle

A CT scanner sends X-rays through the head from many angles while it rotates, and a computer turns the measurements into thin slices. Bone looks white, fluid dark, and fresh blood bright. CT is fast — a head scan takes seconds — so it is the first test for an emergency such as a suspected stroke, to check whether there is bleeding. It does use a small dose of radiation. Godfrey Hounsfield and Allan Cormack shared the 1979 Nobel Prize for it.

MRI: a magnet and radio waves

An MRI scanner uses a very strong magnet — usually 1.5 or 3 tesla, tens of thousands of times stronger than Earth’s field. It lines up the hydrogen nuclei in the water and fat of your body, knocks them with radio waves and listens as they settle back. Different tissues settle at different speeds, which creates the image. MRI uses no X-rays, shows soft tissue in great detail and is loud. Metal must stay out of the room. Paul Lauterbur and Peter Mansfield won the 2003 Nobel Prize for it.

T1, T2, FLAIR…

By changing the timing of the radio pulses, MRI can make different tissues bright. In T1 images fat is bright and anatomy is clear; in T2 images fluid is bright; FLAIR darkens the fluid around the brain so damaged areas stand out; diffusion images show a stroke within minutes. Functional MRI (fMRI) follows blood oxygen to see which areas are working.
