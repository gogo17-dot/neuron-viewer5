Neuron cell

The neuron is the nervous system’s signalling unit: a cell body (soma) often on the order of tens of micrometres across in cortex, branching dendrites that collect thousands of synaptic inputs, a single axon that can range from millimetres to more than a metre in humans, and axon terminals that pass the signal to the next cell. Chemical synapse

A chemical synapse is the communication gap between neurons: the presynaptic side packages neurotransmitter in vesicles and releases it at the active zone; molecules cross a synaptic cleft only about tens of nanometres wide; the postsynaptic membrane holds receptors and scaffolding (including the postsynaptic density) that convert binding into electrical and biochemical changes in the receiving cell. This scene highlights why that short distance and precise alignment make transmission fast and reliable.

Meninges

The meninges are the three protective membranes enveloping the brain and spinal cord — dura, arachnoid, and pia — that cushion CNS tissue, carry venous channels, and form dural folds such as the falx cerebri and tentorium cerebelli. Diencephalon

The diencephalon sits deep between the cerebral hemispheres and brainstem: thalamus as the great sensory and motor relay to cortex, hypothalamus linking autonomic and endocrine control, mammillary bodies in memory circuits, habenula in reward and aversion pathways, and geniculate bodies plus optic radiations for vision. Ventricles

The ventricular system is a continuous CSF space — lateral ventricles, third ventricle, cerebral aqueduct, and fourth ventricle — lined in places by choroid plexus that secretes cerebrospinal fluid. CSF cushions the brain, clears metabolites, and transmits pressure; blockages and overproduction reshape the ventricles. White matter

White matter is the brain’s cabling: myelinated axons bundled into commissures and long tracts that link distant cortex and deep nuclei. Major pathways here include the corpus callosum, fornix, cerebral peduncles, and optic radiations — each with a distinct course and clinical vulnerability. Limbic system

The limbic system is a set of cortical and deep structures that bind memory, emotion, and motivation: hippocampus for episodic encoding, amygdala for threat and salience, cingulate cortex for affect and attention, fornix as a major output path, and septal nuclei in reward circuitry. Cranial nerves

The twelve cranial nerves leave the brainstem and skull base to serve smell, vision, eye movement, facial sensation and expression, hearing and balance, swallowing, and tongue and neck control. Visual pathway

The visual pathway carries retinal signals through the optic nerve, chiasm, tract, lateral geniculate nucleus, and optic radiations into primary visual cortex. Crossing at the chiasm and the long course of the radiations mean lesions at each station produce classic field cuts — from monocular loss to bitemporal hemianopia to homonymous defects. Auditory pathway

Hearing begins at the auricle and middle-ear ossicles, becomes a neural code in the cochlea, then ascends through brainstem relays to auditory cortex — all nested in temporal-bone and brain context. This pathway explains conductive versus sensorineural loss, brainstem auditory evoked potentials, and where tumours or infarcts silence sound. Brainstem

The brainstem — midbrain, pons, and medulla — is a compact corridor for consciousness, breathing, cranial-nerve nuclei, and the long ascending and descending tracts. Tiny lesions here can devastate wakefulness, eye movement, facial strength, and vital autonomic control. Basal ganglia

The basal ganglia are deep nuclei that select and scale movement: caudate and putamen as striatum (the input station), globus pallidus as output with the substantia nigra, and the subthalamic nucleus driving the indirect pathway. Cerebellum

The cerebellum tunes movement rather than commanding it: vermis and hemispheric lobules, three peduncles of traffic in and out, and deep nuclei (dentate, interposed, fastigial) that send the real output.
