How does the brain work?

4 min read · AXON

About 1.4 kg, 86 billion neurons and a fifth of your body’s energy. A short tour of the organ that makes you you.

Built from neurons

The brain is made of nerve cells called neurons — about 86 billion of them — plus a similar number of support cells called glia. Each neuron connects to thousands of others, so the brain is less like a single machine and more like a huge network that is always talking to itself.

Neurons talk with electricity and chemistry. A signal runs along a neuron as an electrical pulse, and at the end it is passed on to the next cell by chemical messengers called neurotransmitters.

The big parts

The cerebrum is the largest part. Its wrinkled outer layer, the cortex, is split into lobes: the frontal lobe plans and decides and moves your body, the parietal lobe feels touch and knows where your body is, the temporal lobe hears and helps you remember, and the occipital lobe sees.

Under the cortex sit deeper structures: the thalamus, a relay station for the senses; the hypothalamus, which controls hunger, temperature and hormones; and the basal ganglia, which help start and smooth out movement. At the back, the cerebellum fine-tunes balance and coordination, and the brainstem connects everything to the spinal cord and keeps you breathing.

Left side, right side

Most movement and touch pathways cross the midline, so the left half of the brain controls the right half of the body and the other way round. That is why a stroke on one side of the brain weakens the opposite side of the body.

Always changing

Every time you learn something, connections between neurons get stronger or weaker. This ability to change, called plasticity, is how practice turns into skill — and how the brain can partly recover after an injury.
